## 1. Questions

Can an LLM grade AI4ALL Ignite capstone presentations with the existing instructor rubric? Is repeated grading consistent? Do the grades align with historical instructors? Does post-hoc calibration help without erasing real differences between students?

Three goals are kept separate:
1. **Reliability (consistency).** Does the same setup give the same grade when asked again?
2. **Historical alignment.** Does the grade agree with the one the instructor recorded?
3. **Defensibility (validity).** Can the grade be justified from verifiable evidence and the rubric? Matching an instructor does not prove a grade is justified, and a consistent grade is not necessarily a correct one.

Also asked:
- Is an instructor-like prompt better than a literal-rubric prompt?
- Does a fixed, criterion-specific calibration fitted on development decks transfer to the locked test decks?
- Does calibration inflate scores at the top?
- Can the grader still tell weaker work apart?

## 3. Design and prompts

| | No calibration | Criterion-specific calibration |
|---|---|---|
| **Strict rubric prompt** | **A** · Strict rubric | **B** · Strict rubric + calibration |
| **Instructor-like prompt** | **C** · Human-like | **D** · Human-like + calibration |

- **Scoring:** both prompts scored all 198 decks, each criterion separately, **10 runs per deck and criterion**. That is 9,900 outputs per prompt and 19,800 in total. Every run returned an integer 1–5.
- **Calibration:** a fixed lookup table per criterion and prompt, applied to each of the 10 outputs. It is deterministic, so it needed no extra LLM calls.
- **Summary grade:** the **mode of the 10 runs**; no ties occurred, before or after calibration. A project total is the sum of its five criterion modes. The 10 runs must never be summed as if they were independent students.
- **Fitting:** both tables are fitted on the **46 development decks only** with Phase 1's registered method, then evaluated on the 84 locked test decks. The method takes the median instructor grade for each raw grade and makes it monotone (a higher raw grade never maps lower, and outputs are capped at 5). A raw grade with no development decks is filled by the mean development shift.
  - **Strict table:** reproduces Phase 1's registered table exactly.
  - **Human-like table:** refitted in Revision 1 (`calibration/human_like_table_dev_fit.json`).

**The instructor-like prompt** ([`prompts/human_like_prompt.py`](prompts/human_like_prompt.py), verbatim) asks for the grade "a thoughtful, supportive, experienced human instructor would reasonably give". Three things stand out:
1. **It is generous by design.** Its guidelines tell the model to credit understanding shown implicitly, not to over-penalise minor omissions, and to "reserve scores of 1–3 for projects with meaningful gaps". Unlike the strict scorer, it encodes the leniency that calibration otherwise has to add.
2. **It returns a bare integer:** no rationale, no cited evidence, no abstention. Arms C and D therefore can't meet the evidence requirements in §12.
3. **It takes only extracted text, one criterion per call.**

The strict prompt used in Phase 2 was not supplied. Its grades match Phase 1's strict scorer on 98% of deck-criterion pairs ([`VERIFICATION.md`](VERIFICATION.md) §2).

| Algorithm | [2, 4, 4, 5, 5] | [**1**, **2**, 3, 5, 5] | 0, 0, 3, 15, 28 |
| Essential Question | [2, 4, 4, 5, 5] | [3, 3, 4, 4, 5] | 2, 1, 9, 19, 15 |

| Citations | [3, 3, 3, 4, 5] | [**1**, 3, 3, 4, 5] | 0, 2, 10, 12, 22 |

**Strict prompt:** conservative compared with instructors. Its table raises Algorithm and Essential Question sharply, sets a floor of 4 on Next Steps (raw 1–4 all become 4) and a floor of 3 on Citations. That improves alignment with lenient historical grading but removes most of the differences between decks.

**Human-like prompt:** already generous, so its table is light:
- Project Description: 3 → 4.
- Algorithm: 4 → 5.
- Essential Question: a floor of 3, and 3 → 4.
- Next Steps: 3 → 4.
- Citations: 2 → 3.

Both tables are transparent, frozen post-processing rules learned to mimic historical grades, not a statement of ideal grading.

## 5. Main results · locked test set

All five criteria pooled: **420 comparisons across 84 test projects**. Each uses the mode of 10 runs.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A · Strict | 41.7% (175) | 0.874 | 77.1% | −0.612 | 0.291 | 91.2% | 97.2% |
| B · Strict + cal | 52.6% (221) | 0.595 | 89.8% | −0.014 | 0.270 | 95.5% | 98.7% |
| C · Human-like | 47.6% (200) | 0.683 | 86.4% | −0.207 | 0.289 | 89.5% | 97.3% |

- **Calibration helps both prompts out of sample.** It lowers MAE and raises exact agreement for each.
- **After calibration the two prompts are statistically indistinguishable.** B and D give the **same grade on 91% of test comparisons** (381/420), even though their raw prompts agree on only 62% (261/420). On Project Description, Next Steps and Citations they agree on every test deck. Calibration maps both prompts onto essentially the same instructor-style grade.
- **The instructor-like prompt is better before calibration** (C vs A), because it builds in leniency that calibration otherwise supplies.
- **Neither calibrated arm beats "always 5" on exact agreement.** Both beat it on MAE (0.595 and 0.607 vs 0.626) and on within ±1.
- **None of this proves validity.**
  - QWK stays low (about 0.27–0.29) and falls after calibration for both prompts (0.291 → 0.270 and 0.289 → 0.266).
  - Calibration turns under-grading into over-grading where instructors gave low grades (§7).

## 9. Repeatability · all 198 projects (990 deck-criterion groups)

<!-- auto:repeat -->
| Arm | All 10 runs identical | Mean pairwise agreement | Mean modal share |
|---|---|---|---|
| A · Strict | 892 (90.1%) | 97.0% | 98.3% |
| B · Strict + cal | 930 (93.9%) | 98.2% | 99.0% |
| C · Human-like | 859 (86.8%) | 96.6% | 98.2% |
| D · Human-like + cal | 903 (91.2%) | 97.9% | 98.9% |
<!-- /auto:repeat -->

**Definitions:**
- **Mode:** the most frequent of the 10 grades.
- **Unanimous:** all 10 grades identical.
- **Pairwise agreement:** the share of the 45 possible pairs of runs that agree. For example, nine 4s and one 5 give 36/45 = 80%.
- **Modal share:** the count of the most common grade divided by 10.

**Calibration doesn't make the model more deterministic.** Merging grades (2, 3 and 4 all becoming 4, say) mechanically raises agreement among the *reported* grades.

**"True mode" vs sample mode.**
- The **true mode** is the grade the model is most likely to give for a fixed input, model, prompt and settings. It is not the student's true grade, and it can't be observed directly. The 10-run mode estimates it.
- **Batch repeatability** is a different quantity: the chance that another 10-run batch gives the same mode. An illustrative Bayesian example with five identical 4s gave about 93% for "4 is the true mode" but only 64.3% for "a new batch repeats it". That example illustrates the distinction; it is not a confidence figure for every deck.
- Even 10/10 identical runs give an exact one-sided 95% lower bound of about **74%** on the underlying probability, assuming independent, identical draws.

**Stopping rule (exploratory):** "stop after three identical grades in a row" reproduced the 10-run mode in **990/990** strict sequences, using about **3.11 calls** on average. That is an observation about these recorded sequences, not proof of future correctness. Before relying on it, test it on new independent runs, with the confidence required and tolerable loss stated up front.

## 12. Defensibility · established and not established

Established: **repeatability**, and **agreement with a historical reference**. Not yet established: **evidence-grounded validity** on an audited set. Defensibility needs, for every criterion scored:
1. **A rubric-based rationale** that names the requirement being judged.
2. **Verifiable evidence**: a quote, slide number, citation, or a specific missing element. The model must not invent evidence or credit what it cannot see.
3. **Source-access checks.** Flag visuals, notes, data sources or speech that text extraction may have dropped. Absence from the extracted text is not absence from the project.
4. **Versioned provenance:** project ID, artifact hash, rubric version, prompt, model, settings, the 10 raw outputs, calibration-table version, final grade, and any human override.
5. **A human-review path** for missing evidence, extraction problems, unstable grades, large gaps (2 points or more) and other known failure cases.
6. **Separate acceptance thresholds** for reliability, alignment, evidence, subgroup consistency and educational consequences. Passing one does not waive another.

The instructor-like prompt returns only an integer, so it can't meet points 1–3. Phase 1's strict scorer was built to meet them: it cites slides, records a rationale and a checklist, and abstains when content can't be seen.

**Unresolved construct-validity question:** rubric strictness against instructor generosity. Both may be legitimate conventions. A model fitted to historical grades can learn instructors' omissions, credit for the live presentation, and leniency. Keep both the raw strict and the instructor-aligned grades visible to reviewers. Subgroup fairness was **not** studied, and should be checked only where ethical, authorised and adequately powered.

## 13. Conclusions

1. **The 2 × 2 study is complete:** 46 development and 84 locked test decks.
2. **Grading is highly repeatable.** All 10 raw strict runs agreed on about 90% of deck-criterion groups. Stable is not the same as accurate or defensible.
3. **The instructor-like prompt beats the strict prompt before calibration** on MAE and exact agreement, because it builds in the leniency that calibration otherwise adds.
4. **Calibration fitted on development decks only transfers to the locked test set for both prompts.** Exact agreement +11.0 pp for strict, +3.8 pp for human-like; MAE −0.28 and −0.08.
5. **After calibration the prompt choice washes out.** B and D give the same grade on 91% of test comparisons and don't differ significantly on any measure: exact 52.6% vs 51.4%, MAE 0.595 vs 0.607, bias about −0.015 for both.
6. **Neither calibrated arm beats "always 5" on exact agreement** (57.4%). Both beat it on MAE, within ±1 and project-total error.
7. **Calibration compresses grades upward** and badly misses instructor grades of 1–3: 13.8% (B) and 15.5% (D) exact, against 53.4% for the uncalibrated strict prompt. This is a major validity concern, not a footnote.
8. **Total correlations are modest and fall with calibration.** Agreeing on average and telling individuals apart are different outcomes.
9. **Choose the prompt on defensibility, not alignment.** Calibrated alignment is the same either way, so prefer the strict, evidence-citing scorer that can abstain. Report the raw strict grade alongside any calibrated one.
10. **Not ready for autonomous grading.** The most valuable next step is:
    - an instructor-adjudicated evidence audit;
    - a new external or later holdout;
    - safeguards for low grades and for missing visual or text evidence.

    More repeat calls are not the priority.

## 14. Continuation guidance

- Treat this phase's results and the private joined table as the completed state. **Do not rerun the expensive LLM grading** to reconstruct them; recalibration needs no LLM calls.
- Keep all four arms. Base primary claims on the 84-deck locked test, and use the 68 unlabelled decks only for repeatability.
- Always report the "always 5" baseline alongside the arms.
- Prioritise defensibility, the true-mode vs sample-mode distinction, low-grade detection, and rubric-versus-instructor differences.
- The earlier slide deck *AI_Grading_Expanded_198_Project_Pilot* mixed pooled comparisons from before the split was settled. Use this document's split-based results instead.
