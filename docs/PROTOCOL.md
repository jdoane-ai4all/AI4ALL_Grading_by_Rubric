# Study Protocol: Can rubric-aligned AI scoring support human review of student AI/ML project presentations?

**Status:** design pre-registered on 2026-11-02. **No model has scored any real artifact yet. Nothing in this repository is an empirical result** except the audit counts in §3, which the PI supplied and which must be revalidated against the live workbook.
**Principal investigator:** Jonathan Doane, Ph.D. This is an independent candidate research project using AI4ALL Ignite program artifacts. It is not sponsored by any prospective employer.
**Governance:** student work stays private. AI scores are research outputs and never change any participant's historical grade or any program decision.

---

## 1. Executive brief

**Question.** Under what conditions can rubric-aligned LLM scoring of redacted final-presentation slide decks reproduce trained instructors' historical criterion scores? Where is additional human review required?

**Why it matters.** The rubric has six criteria, each scored 1 to 5. The nomination threshold is 24/30. This is a typical low-stakes, constructed-response scoring task. The useful product decision is not whether AI can grade. It is which parts of the scoring a model can pre-fill reliably, which parts need a person, and how we would know.

**Minimum viable study (pilot, about 2 weeks part-time, about $60–100 API).**
1. Revalidate the audit. Freeze the label gates. Create the grouped, stratified development/test split (§6.4).
2. Score the **development** set with the same model under two prompts: basic (C1) and evidence-grounded (C2). Use one call per criterion.
3. Report per-criterion error patterns, abstentions and run-to-run stability on development data only. Draft review-policy thresholds.
4. Stop/go gate G2 (§14) decides whether the locked test set is worth spending.

**Full study (about 6–8 weeks, about $400–600 API, optional human rescoring).** Score the locked test set once under the frozen C1–C4 conditions, with three replicates of the primary condition. Add the optional blinded same-artifact human rescoring (§13).

**What a "good" result looks like.** It doesn't mean high agreement everywhere. It means a defensible map of where the AI agrees within tolerance, where it fails, and whether a cheap routing policy can catch the failures. A well-characterised negative result is a publishable and decision-useful outcome.

## 2. Estimands (kept separate throughout)

| ID | Estimand | Reference scores | Available now? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **E1** | Agreement between AI scores and **historical instructor scores**, for the same projects | Historical Ed STEM rubric scores (provisional, not adjudicated truth) | Yes |
| **E2** | Agreement between AI scores and **independent human ratings of the same redacted artifact** | New blinded ratings (§13) | Only if raters are recruited |
| **E3** | Within-model repeatability (the same input scored repeatedly) | AI vs itself | Yes |
| **E4** | Whether a review policy keeps serious errors among auto-accepted cases below tolerance, and at what review workload | E1 references, applied to the locked test set | Yes |

**Why E1 and E2 differ.** Historical scores were assigned by instructors who saw the oral presentation, any demo, unredacted names, links and their own program context. The AI sees only a redacted PDF. E1 disagreement can therefore reflect AI error, information the AI never had, ordinary rater variation, or label noise. E2 holds the evidence base constant. Until E2 exists, E1 results are framed as "reproducing the historical grading outcome from the artifact alone", and the gap between E1 and E2 is itself a finding.

### 3.4 Findings from reading the rubric source document that change the design
1. **Two wordings of the essential question exist.** The rubric document asks: "How can the process of creating AI/ML solutions amplify or mitigate bias in the case our group is using?" The program statement asks: "How can the development processes of responsible AI/ML solutions address and mitigate the technology's negative impacts while strengthening its positive effects?" Which cohorts received which wording is unknown, so the scorer is told to accept either. *Decision log D-07.*
2. **The zero rule.** The source says: "No submission, missing names, or lack of citations are graded as a zero." This has two consequences.
   - (a) The scorer must never apply it, because names are redacted.
   - (b) Some of the 207 all-zero source records may be real zeros for missing citations rather than "not graded". If so, the weakest projects are disproportionately missing from the labelled set, and the eligible sample is **truncated at the bottom**. That would make the ceiling effect partly a selection artefact, and E1 says little about the weakest work. *Mitigation:* report it, compare the extraction features (page count, presence of a references slide) of labelled vs unlabelled artifacts descriptively, and score unlabelled artifacts for abstention behaviour only.
3. **Requirement vs rubric mismatch on citations.** The assignment requires "at least 3 to 4 citations", while the rubric awards 5 only for 7 or more. Students who met the stated requirement land on 3. This is construct-relevant and is not changed, but it explains part of the Citations distribution.
4. **"Well beyond the content covered in the program"** (Essential Question, level 5) can't be observed from the artifact. The scorer flags `requires_program_context` instead of guessing (§6.3).
5. **"Demo of the model", "evaluation of the model", "positive and negative impact" and "GitHub link"** are requirements but not scored criteria. They are not scored, and they are not used as proxies.

### 3.5 Artifact-equivalence and label-quality gates
These are applied before the split and never relaxed afterwards. A label is eligible only if it meets all of the following:
- complete 1–5 vector;
- reported total equals the sum of the six scores;
- not in conflict with another record for the same artifact;
- the graded submission's resource ID matches the PDF (already verified);
- the artifact is not a watermarked draft that differs materially from the graded version (checklist in `docs/DATA_ISSUES_AND_AUDIT.md`).

Labels are never averaged, stitched, majority-voted or chosen as "first record".

## 4. Claim / evidence / decision matrix

| Claim we might want to make | Evidence that would support it | Evidence that would disconfirm it | Decision it informs |
|---|---|---|---|
| C-a: AI reproduces historical criterion scores within tolerance for criterion *k* | Test MAE ≤ 0.5 (upper CI noted), within-one ≥ 0.90, severe rate ≤ 5%, no systematic signed bias | Severe errors concentrated in low-scored work; bias of 0.3 or more; coverage under 90% | Whether criterion *k* can be AI-pre-filled for human confirmation |
| C-b: Evidence-grounded prompting improves on a basic prompt | Paired test MAE difference CI excludes 0, in favour of C2 | CI includes 0, or C2 is worse | Whether to keep the more expensive prompt |
| C-c: Visual input matters for visual criteria | C2 beats C3 on Data Visualization; no difference on text criteria | No difference on Data Visualization | Input pipeline cost |
| C-d: Joint scoring induces halo | AI inter-criterion correlations under C4 exceed both C2's and the historical correlations | Similar correlation matrices | Call organisation |
| C-e: Scores are stable enough to audit | Any-change rate across replicates is low; range ≥ 2 is rare | Frequent flips | Need for replicate voting or human review |
| C-f: A routing policy makes AI support safe at acceptable workload | Among auto-accepted test projects, severe-error and threshold-misclassification rates are below tolerance at ≥ 40% coverage | Tolerances unmet at any useful coverage | Whether a human-in-the-loop workflow pilot is justified |
| **Not claimable from this design** | — | — | Fairness across demographic groups (no data); time savings (no workflow study); learning benefit; validity of the total as a single latent trait; generalisation to new cohorts or instructors |

Agreement, even when perfect, is necessary but not sufficient evidence for valid use (see the Standards' treatment of automated scoring documentation and the Williamson, Xi & Breyer framework, §16).

## 6. Design

### 6.1 Conditions (same model and settings across all conditions)

| Condition | Prompt | Input | Organisation | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C0 | — | — | — | Trivial baseline: modal score per criterion from the **development** set only |
| C1 | Basic: rubric plus "give a level" | PDF | One call per criterion | Reference AI condition |
| **C2** | Evidence-grounded: rubric, criterion procedure, page citations, abstention rules | PDF | One call per criterion | **Primary**; 3 replicates |
| C3 | Evidence-grounded | Extracted text only | One call per criterion | Modality ablation |
| C4 | Evidence-grounded | PDF | All six in one call | Halo / organisation ablation |

Each contrast changes exactly one factor. Model: `claude-opus-5-5`, effort `medium`, adaptive thinking. Thinking can't be disabled on this model. Thinking is neither requested in readable form nor stored. **Server-side model fallback is deliberately disabled**, because a silent switch to another model would confound the comparison. Refusals are logged and counted as failures. The served model ID is logged on every call.

**Why one call per criterion is primary.**
- It isolates each criterion's construct and reduces halo effects.
- It allows criterion-specific procedures and per-criterion abstention.
- It makes failures traceable.

The deck is cached and shared across the six calls, so the extra cost is small. C4 measures the cost of the cheaper alternative.

**Why there is no fine-tuning or supervised model.** 128 labels with 44–77% at the ceiling cannot support it. A trained model would mostly learn to predict 5. The study evaluates inference-time scoring, which matches how such features are usually built first.

**Exemplars.** v1 is zero-shot, because the rubric's level descriptors are the anchors. A few-shot variant may be explored on development data only. Exemplars would be chosen deliberately, one per level per criterion, with their order varied, and any exemplar project would be excluded from every test analysis. Exemplars are never chosen at random, because random selection mostly yields 5s.

### 6.2 Scorer input allowlist (enforced in code, `src/rubric_scoring/inputs.py`)
**Allowed:** the frozen system prompt (rubric and rules), the redacted deck (PDF bytes, or page-marked text), and the criterion task.

**Forbidden:** project ID, title metadata, filenames, cohort, tutorial, style, historical scores, audit or linkage fields, source row numbers, `Student_Grade_Lookup`, roster data, self-reflections, and administrative flags.

Joining happens only after scoring, through identifiers stored outside the model context. Every request passes `assert_no_leakage()`. Tests check that the project ID never appears in a request.

### 6.3 Operational rubric guidance (evidence condition)
The criterion procedures are in `src/rubric_scoring/prompts.py::OPERATIONAL_GUIDANCE`, and the same text goes to human raters in §13. Key operational decisions (decision log):
- **Data Visualization:** count distinct charts, plots, heatmaps, confusion matrices, ROC and feature-importance plots, and maps showing data. Do not count logos, stock images, code screenshots, or architecture diagrams without data. Plain tables are listed as borderline and not counted. Duplicates count once.
- **Citations:** list entries with pages, mark topic relevance and whether each identifies the data source, then count. Candidate counts from automated extraction are never converted into grades. Redacted or unreadable reference slides lead to ABSTAIN, not to a score of 1.
- **Essential Question 4 vs 5:** if the decision depends only on program context, the scorer picks the level the deck otherwise supports and flags `requires_program_context`. These cases are analysed separately.
- **Observability rule:** content clearly absent from a complete artifact is scored by the rubric. Content made unobservable by redaction, extraction failure or reliance on oral delivery or a demo leads to ABSTAIN with a reason.

### 6.4 Splitting (`src/rubric_scoring/split.py`)
- **Unit:** provisional duplicate/version group, so all versions and pages of a project stay together.
- **Strata:** cohort × total band, with bands below 24 (below the nomination threshold), 24–28, and 29–30.
- **Proportions:** 35% development and 65% test, seeded, with largest-remainder allocation. Development only needs to expose prompt failures, while the test set carries the inferential load. §6.5 shows the precision cost.
- **Registry:** `registry/split_registry.json` records assignments, strata and a SHA-256 hash, and it refuses to be overwritten. Only the hash is committed publicly.
- **Exposure disclosure:** aggregate label distributions were inspected during data QA before the split. No prompt, exemplar, calibration or threshold may be tuned on test outcomes.
- **Cohort holdout:** it was considered and not adopted as primary. FA25 has about 30 labelled groups, too few for a stable held-out estimate. A leave-one-cohort-out analysis is reported descriptively in the test results. Generalisation to new cohorts or instructors is not claimed.

### 6.5 Precision (SIMULATED; see `docs/SIMULATED_PRECISION.md`)
- With about 82 test groups, the 95% width of per-criterion MAE is roughly 0.18–0.28, depending on the AI error scenario. That is enough to distinguish an MAE of about 0.25 from the 0.5 tolerance.
- Quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) widths are 0.15–0.42 and swing strongly with the ceiling. QWK is therefore secondary.
- Expected human scores of 3 or below per criterion in the test set range from about 7 (Data Visualization) to about 18 (Next Steps). Over-scoring of weak work can be **described** but not precisely estimated, and that is reported as a limitation.
- Moving more groups into development would cut these test counts further. That is the reason for the 35/65 split.

## 7. Metrics, tolerances and uncertainty

**Primary (per criterion, locked test set, condition C2, replicate 1):** MAE against the historical score, with a 95% project-group cluster-bootstrap CI (5,000 resamples).

**Secondary, per criterion:**
- exact and within-one agreement;
- severe-discrepancy rate (|difference| ≥ 2);
- signed bias;
- full 5×5 confusion matrix;
- QWK (primary weights), with linear weighted kappa as sensitivity. Both are reported with prevalence caveats, because kappa is unstable when 77% of cases sit in one category;
- over-scoring rate among human scores of 3 or below (descriptive).

**Totals (projects with all six criteria scored; denominator reported):**
- MAE; signed bias;
- ICC(A,1): two-way, absolute agreement, single measure (McGraw & Wong, 1996), with the AI treated as a fixed source. The model, unit and bootstrap CI are stated;
- Bland–Altman mean difference and limits of agreement;
- agreement at the 24/30 threshold, with false-nominate and false-exclude rates;
- **cancellation ratio**: the share of summed criterion-level error that disappears in the total. A good total MAE can hide offsetting criterion errors. Pearson r is reported as descriptive only, because correlation is not agreement.

**Coverage:** the share of calls producing a valid score is reported next to every agreement metric. Abstentions and failures are never silently dropped. A sensitivity analysis imputes the worst case for non-scored calls.

**Paired comparisons:** differences in MAE between conditions on the same projects, with bootstrap CIs on the same resampled groups. Holm adjustment is applied across the three pre-specified contrasts. Per-criterion contrasts are exploratory.

**Non-independence:** the six criteria, the replicates and duplicate artifacts are never pooled to inflate N. Pooled-criterion summaries are labelled descriptive.

**Pre-specified tolerances for an "AI pre-fills, human confirms" use** (`config/study_config.json`; a program lead reviews them before unlock, and they are not edited after):
- criterion MAE ≤ 0.5;
- within-one agreement ≥ 0.90;
- severe-discrepancy rate ≤ 5%;
- threshold agreement ≥ 0.85.

These are judgement calls for a low-stakes, human-confirmed use. They are not psychometric constants.

## 8. Repeated-run stability (E3)
The primary condition is run three times on identical inputs. Each run logs: model requested and served, effort, prompt hash, input SHA-256, timestamp and order. Reported measures:
- the share of project-criterion pairs whose score changed;
- abstain/score flips;
- the share of pairs with a range of 2 or more.

Stability is reported separately from agreement. A stable model can be stably wrong. Stability does **not** say anything about human rater reliability.

## 9. Targeted validity and robustness checks (development set first)

| Check | Method | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| Chart interpretation | Evidence pages for Data Visualization vs PI-verified counts on 20 decks | Whether counts reflect real visualizations |
| Algorithm explanation | Review whether flagged "errors" are real | Construct-relevant scoring |
| Deadlines in Next Steps | Checklist item "deadlines" vs deck text | Whether level 5 is reached for the right reason |
| Citation and data-source verification | Scorer-listed citations vs a manual list on 20 decks | Whether counts are faithful and relatedness judgments hold |
| Writing polish | Controlled variant: the same content with typos introduced or removed (same group, never in test metrics) | Whether score shifts reflect cosmetic features |
| Layout | Controlled variant: the same content re-rendered in a plain template | Visual-design sensitivity |
| Redaction and OCR | Compare abstention rates on the 124 low-OCR-confidence pages and on redacted reference slides | Whether the observability rule works |
| Performance level | Error by human score level, descriptive only | Conditioning on a noisy human score can create regression artefacts, so no causal reading |

Controlled variants preserve substantive content, stay inside their project's group, and are never counted as new projects.

## 10. Fairness: what this study can and cannot say
- No verified protected-attribute data are in the analysis table, and none will be inferred from names, faces, topics, dialect or writing style.
- Cohort, format and style analyses are **process-robustness** checks. They are not evidence of demographic fairness.
- **Proposed extension (separately governed):** if AI4ALL holds consented, verified demographic data, an approved analyst could compute aggregate differential-error statistics (signed bias and severe-error rates by group) with uncertainty, minimum cell sizes (e.g. n ≥ 20 groups) and multiple-comparison control. Only aggregates would leave the governed environment. This needs program and data-governance approval first.

## 11. Human-review policy (E4)

### 11.1 Rules (`src/rubric_scoring/review_policy.py`)
A project goes to a human if any of the following hold:
- an abstention or invalid output on any criterion;
- run-to-run disagreement;
- a predicted total within ±2 of 24;
- any predicted criterion ≤ 3, since these are rare and carry the most risk;
- a limiting-factor flag.

Thresholds are tuned on the development coverage–risk grid, then frozen.

### 11.2 Reported on test
- coverage (the share auto-accepted);
- review workload;
- severe-error rate among accepted projects;
- criterion error rate among accepted projects;
- threshold misclassification among accepted projects.

Model self-reported confidence is not used as calibrated certainty.

### 11.3 Authority and failure handling
A human holds final authority in every configuration. If an AI scoring call fails, the item falls back to standard human grading, with no degraded automatic score. A later **timed, controlled workflow study** (instructors scoring with and without AI pre-fill, crossed design, measuring time, agreement with adjudicated scores, and automation bias through seeded AI errors) is the only way to support claims about time saved. This study does not make them.

## 12. Psychometric models: where they are and are not justified
- **Generalizability theory** needs crossed or partially crossed facets. Here instructors are nested in their own students, and there is one historical rating per project, so a person × rater G-study is **not estimable**. With the §13 extension (2–3 raters crossed on a subset), a p × r design per criterion becomes estimable, and a p × r × c design could describe the criterion facet. The model run could enter as a separate facet for E3.
- **Many-facet Rasch** has the same problem. Rater severity is confounded with cohort and project quality without overlap. It is estimable only with anchor/overlap ratings and would then help separate AI severity from human severity.
- **IRT on the six criteria** is not justified as a test of unidimensionality. The criteria are distinct requirements of a deliverable, not interchangeable items, and internal consistency (α) is not evidence that the total measures one ability. Correlations among criteria are reported only as halo diagnostics (C-d).
- **What is used:** ordinal agreement statistics, cluster bootstrap and ICC(A,1) for totals. These answer the questions actually asked.

## 13. Optional extension: blinded same-artifact human rescoring (E2)
- **Design:** 40 test-set groups (stratified like the split) plus 10 development groups for rater training. Three trained raters, each project scored by 2 of the 3 in a balanced incomplete design, so all rater pairs overlap. Raters use the same redacted PDFs, the same rubric and the same operational guidance, and never see historical or AI scores. Independent ratings are saved before any adjudication. Adjudication output is stored separately.
- **What it adds:**
  - human–human agreement on identical evidence, as a ceiling for AI agreement;
  - the E1-versus-E2 gap, which shows how much disagreement comes from missing oral or context information;
  - estimable rater effects (a p × r G-study).
- **Cost:** about 20 minutes per deck × 2 ratings × 40 decks ≈ 27 hours, plus about 6 hours of training and calibration and about 4 hours of adjudication. Roughly 35–40 rater-hours, which at typical contract rates is about $1.4k–$2.4k. The core study does not depend on it.
- **Form:** `docs/templates/human_rating_form.csv`.

## 15. Limitations
1. Historical scores are a single provisional rating per project, by the student's own instructor, informed by oral and contextual evidence the AI lacks. "No measurable difference in grading styles" is a **working assumption, not a measured result**, and it is untestable without overlap.
2. Missing labels are probably not random (§3.4, item 2). Results may not apply to the weakest projects.
3. Ceiling effects limit discrimination and make kappa unstable. Low-score error is described, not precisely estimated.
4. Decks are redacted, and some evidence (links, QR codes, references) may be missing. Observability rules reduce but don't remove this.
5. One model family at one point in time; model updates can change behaviour. The served model ID is logged, but results do not transfer automatically to other versions.
6. Possible training-data exposure of public student repositories can't be ruled out. It would inflate agreement only if the model had seen the grades, which is unlikely, but it is noted.
7. Agreement does not establish validity, fairness, time savings or student benefit.
8. Aggregate label distributions were seen before the split.

## 18. Pre-registered calibration analysis (Option B, registered 2026-11-02 before any test deck was scored)

**Aim:** decompose agreement with historical grades into (i) a portable, rubric-literal scorer and (ii) a small organisation-specific "leniency layer" learned from locally graded examples.

- **B0 literal:** F1 scores as produced.
- **B1 additive shift per criterion:** δ_c = mean(historical − AI) on development pairs; calibrated = clip(round(AI + δ_c), 1, 5).
- **B2 monotone mapping table per criterion:** the median historical score at each AI level, made non-decreasing; unseen levels fall back to B1.
- **Primary calibrated method: B2.** B1 is a sensitivity analysis. Both fits are frozen in `config/calibration_fit_dev.json` (fitted on 46 development artifacts only). Abstentions are never converted into scores.

**Test-set reporting:** every metric in §7 is reported for B0, B1 and B2, together with the always-5 baseline (C0). Paired MAE differences (B2 − B0, and B2 − C0) use the same project-group bootstrap.

**Learning curve, to answer "how many locally graded decks does an organisation need?":** B2 is refitted on random subsets of 5, 10, 20 and 30 development groups (200 draws each) and evaluated on the test set. The report gives the median and 2.5–97.5% range of test MAE.

**Interpretation rule:** B2's tables are descriptive of how this organisation's historical grading relates to literal rubric application. They are *not* evidence that the historical scores are more correct, nor that the literal scores are. Without blinded same-artifact human ratings (§13), leniency and information the AI never had (oral presentation, demo) cannot be separated.
